    AI in Your Course

    Case study: a self-paced faculty consultation and workshop for setting generative AI rules per assignment, responding fairly to suspected misuse, and redesigning assignments so learning stays visible.

    Try the demoPlay it on this page

    The problem

    Instructional technology teams get the same request again and again: “Help me with AI in my course.” Behind it are three different problems. Faculty don’t know what to allow, students don’t know what’s allowed, and when an AI detector flags an essay, nobody is sure what the score means.

    One-size policies don’t work, because the right answer depends on what each assignment teaches. A one-off consultation for every instructor doesn’t scale either. I wanted a kit that does the first consultation for them and leaves a clear plan the team can follow up on.

    Goal and action map

    Goal: By the start of term, faculty publish AI rules students can follow for every assignment, and suspected misuse cases start from evidence rather than a detector score.

    Goal

    Clear AI rules per assignment, fair responses

    What people must do
    • Set an AI level from each assignment’s learning goal
    • Publish the statement in Canvas
    • Treat tool output as one signal
    • Redesign one high-risk assignment
    Practice in the demo
    • Choose levels for four assignments
    • Review the generated statement
    • Three-moment misuse case
    • Redesign with a grading-time trade-off
    Minimum information
    • Four AI levels
    • What detectors and exam tools can’t do
    • Canvas placement

    Objectives and evidence

    Planned backward: each objective has matching evidence in the demo.

    #Objective (audience, behavior, condition, degree)Evidence in the demo
    1Given four assignments and their learning goals, a faculty member will choose an AI-use level for each that matches its goal.Step 2: levels with goal-based guidance
    2Given their choices, a faculty member will publish a statement that names the level for each assignment, how to disclose, and what to do when unsure.Step 3: generated statement and Canvas placement
    3Given a flagged essay, a faculty member will choose an evidence-first response in all three moments of the case.Step 4: misuse case
    4For one assignment, a faculty member will select at least two strategies that make learning visible, and weigh the grading time.Step 5: redesign with trade-off meters

    Design decisions

    Start from the learning goal

    Every assignment shows its goal before the AI levels, so the choice follows from what students should learn, not from the tool.

    Four levels, not yes or no

    No AI, AI for ideas, AI for feedback, AI as collaborator. Enough nuance for real courses, few enough to remember.

    A statement written for students

    The generated statement uses plain language, promises that disclosed use won’t be penalized, and tells students to ask when unsure.

    Detectors in context

    The misuse case teaches that a score is a probability, false positives happen, and multilingual writers are flagged more often.

    Visible learning, not AI-proofing

    The redesign step admits nothing is AI-proof and shows the grading-time cost of each strategy.

    Ends in a consultation note

    The last step produces a plan the instructional technology team can follow up on, and a usefulness rating.
